The Government of Kenya sold 15% of Safaricom to Vodacom on 30 June 2026 for KES 204.3 billion. That was a price of KES 34 a share, for an asset every Kenyan taxpayer part-owned.

The state also took KES 40.2 billion up front against the dividends on the 20% it kept. Then, on 15 September, the High Court declared the whole sale void.

**The Afronomics verdict: KES 34 beat the market on the day, but it was a soft price for control, and the dividend advance was dear money.**

## What happened

(Facts) On 4 December 2025 Vodacom announced that it had agreed with the Government of Kenya to buy 15% of Safaricom at KES 34 a share. The buyer is Vodafone Kenya Limited, a Vodacom subsidiary. The stake is 6,009,814,200 shares and the price comes to KES 204.3 billion, according to tech-ish and Mwango Capital. Vodafone Kenya also agreed to pay KES 40.2 billion up front for the right to collect the dividends on the government's remaining 20%, which Mwango Capital describes as a dividend-secured loan. Together that is KES 244.5 billion, Business Daily reports.

(Facts) In the same announcement Vodacom agreed to buy a further 5% from Vodafone at the same KES 34 a share, taking its stake in Safaricom from 35% to 55%. The government holds 20%, down from 35%. Investors on the NSE hold the other 25%, according to tech-ish.

(Facts) By 18 May 2026 the High Court had issued conservatory orders halting the sale while a constitutional petition was heard. On 26 June the Court of Appeal set those orders aside pending the government's intended appeal, according to The Standard. On 29 June the Capital Markets Authority exempted Vodafone Kenya from making a mandatory takeover offer to the other shareholders, under Regulation 5(1) of the 2002 takeover rules, tech-ish reports. The shares changed hands in one block trade on the NSE on 30 June.

(Facts) The government keeps two board seats, according to Mwango Capital, and Vodacom must consult it before expanding outside Kenya. Safaricom stays listed on the NSE, Vodacom's announcement says.

(Facts) On 15 September 2026 a three-judge bench of the High Court declared the sale unconstitutional, null and void, and ordered the shares restored to the government, according to tech-ish. The judges found that the sale was a public policy decision that needed meaningful public participation, and that neither the Cabinet nor the National Assembly had given it that, Citizen Digital reports. Vodacom said it would appeal, according to The Condia. On 13 October the Court of Appeal declined to suspend the judgment pending the government's intended appeal, Citizen Digital reports.

## Was KES 34 a good price?

| Measure | KES a share | KES 34 against it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Close on 2 December 2025, two days before the deal was announced (Mwango Capital) | 28.20 | 20.6% above |
| Standard Investment Bank's fair value (Business Daily) | 40.19 | 15.4% below |
| Price on the day of the 15 September ruling (tech-ish) | 36.50 | 6.8% below |

(Facts) Mwango Capital also put KES 34 at 18.4% above the 90-day and 33.9% above the 180-day volume-weighted average price. Treasury Cabinet Secretary John Mbadi said the Treasury relied on a valuation by KCB Investment Bank and on target prices from investment banks, according to The Star. On Standard Investment Bank's fair value, the government could be leaving about KES 37.1 billion on the table, Business Daily reports.

(Analysis) Against the market, KES 34 was a premium. But the buyer was not buying a few shares. It was buying control of the company, and the minority shareholders on the NSE got no offer at all. A premium of a fifth over a December close is the least the state could have asked for that.

## What the state gives up

(Facts) Safaricom paid KES 2.00 a share for the year to March 2026, a record total of KES 80.13 billion, the company says. Capital FM calls that a 66.7% rise on the year before, which puts the year to March 2025 at KES 1.20. The register for the interim KES 0.85 closed on 25 February 2026, before the sale, The Star reports. The register for the final KES 1.15 closed on 4 August 2026, after it, according to Business Daily.

| On the 15% sold (6.01 billion shares) | KES |
| --- | --- |
| A year's dividend at KES 1.20 (year to March 2025) | 7.2bn |
| A year's dividend at KES 2.00 (year to March 2026) | 12.0bn |
| The interim the state kept (KES 0.85) | 5.1bn |
| The final that went to the buyer (KES 1.15) | 6.9bn |

(Facts) Business Daily reported that had the sale been delayed beyond 4 August, the Treasury would have earned KES 16.1 billion. By our arithmetic that is the KES 1.15 final on the state's whole 35%; the 15% sold accounts for KES 6.9 billion of it. The dividend rights on the 20% it kept were worth KES 55.7 billion as paid, and were sold for KES 40.2 billion, a discount of KES 15.5 billion or 27.8%, according to Business Daily. Vodacom's group financial controller, Shaun Biljon, told analysts that Vodacom discounted three years of expected dividends at an internal rate of return of 16.5%, capped at 18%, Business Daily reports.

(Analysis) At last year's dividend of KES 2.00, the 15% yields about 5.9% a year on KES 34. The one-year Treasury bill sold on 12 October 2026 at 9.04%. Giving up a 5.9% yield to raise money the state would otherwise borrow at about 9% is reasonable arithmetic, if the dividend does not keep growing. It grew two-thirds last year.

(Analysis) The advance is the weaker part. If Vodacom earns 16.5% on it, the state borrowed KES 40.2 billion at a rate well above its own Treasury bill. Until Vodacom has recovered it, the state collects no dividend on its 20%. Vodacom expects that to take two to three years, and Mr Biljon said "just over two years", according to Business Daily.

## Who gains

(Analysis) Vodacom gets control of Safaricom. Within five weeks of the trade, the shares it bought qualified for the KES 1.15 final dividend, about KES 6.9 billion. The Treasury gets KES 244.5 billion in cash. The NSE's minority shareholders keep their shares, but under the CMA exemption reported by tech-ish they get no takeover offer, which the 2002 rules would normally have required.

## Our verdict

(Opinion) **KES 34 beat the market on the day, but it was a soft price for control, and the dividend advance was dear money.**

The sale price was not a giveaway. It was well above where Safaricom traded when it was agreed. The state had a fair case for cash now over dividends later.

But control is worth more than a block of shares, and here the buyer also skipped the offer to everyone else. The dividend advance asked the state to borrow at Vodacom's rate, not its own. And the High Court has found that the process lacked the public participation the constitution requires, a finding the government is challenging. If that challenge fails, someone has to work out how KES 244.5 billion already paid goes back. The government has told the courts the KES 204.3 billion is non-refundable, Citizen Digital reports.

### What would change our mind

The Treasury publishing the KCB Investment Bank valuation in full. A Safaricom dividend that falls rather than rises over the next two years. Or a Court of Appeal judgment that the public consultation was enough.
