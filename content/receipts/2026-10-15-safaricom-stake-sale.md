The Government of Kenya sold 15% of Safaricom to Vodacom on 30 June 2026 for KES 204.3 billion. That was a price of KES 34 a share, for an asset every Kenyan taxpayer part-owned.

The state also took KES 40.2 billion up front against the dividends on the 20% it kept. Then, on 15 September, the High Court declared the whole sale void.

**The Afronomics verdict: KES 34 beat the market on the day, but it was a soft price for control, and the dividend advance was dear money.**

## What happened

(Facts) On 4 December 2025 the National Treasury agreed to sell 6,009,814,200 Safaricom shares, 15% of the company, to Vodafone Kenya Limited, a Vodacom subsidiary, at KES 34 each. Vodacom's announcement put the sale at about KES 204.3 billion. Vodafone Kenya also agreed to pay KES 40.2 billion now for the dividends on the government's remaining 20%, which Business Daily describes as a loan backed by that stake. Together that is KES 244.5 billion.

(Facts) Vodacom also bought out Vodafone Group's share of Vodafone Kenya, the holding company, which adds an effective 5%. After both steps Vodacom controls about 55% of Safaricom. The government holds 20%, down from 35%. Investors on the NSE hold the other 25%.

(Facts) By May 2026 the High Court had frozen the sale. On 26 June the Court of Appeal lifted that order, according to The Standard. On 29 June the Capital Markets Authority exempted Vodafone Kenya from making a takeover offer to the other shareholders, under Regulation 5(1) of the 2002 takeover rules, Techweez reports. The shares changed hands in one block trade on the NSE on 30 June.

(Facts) The government set conditions, according to People Daily: Safaricom's chair and chief executive stay Kenyan, the government keeps two board seats, the brand does not change without its consent, suppliers stay for at least three years, and the company stays listed on the NSE.

(Facts) Treasury Cabinet Secretary John Mbadi said the money went to the new National Infrastructure Fund and will not pay for recurrent spending, People Daily reports. He named the airport at JKIA, roads, irrigation and energy.

(Facts) On 15 September 2026 the High Court declared the sale unconstitutional, null and void, for lack of meaningful public participation, and ordered the shares restored to the government, according to tech-ish. The government and Vodacom have appealed. On 13 October the Court of Appeal refused to suspend that judgment while the appeal is heard, Citizen Digital reports. No date for the appeal hearing has been published.

## Was KES 34 a good price?

| Measure | KES a share | KES 34 against it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Close on 2 December 2025, two days before the deal was announced (Mwango Capital) | 28.20 | 20.6% above |
| Standard Investment Bank's fair value (Business Daily) | 40.19 | 15.4% below |
| Trading around the 15 September ruling (The Condia) | about 36.50 | below |

(Facts) The Treasury says KES 34 was 23.6% above the six-month average price to 2 December 2025. Mr Mbadi said the Treasury relied on a valuation by KCB Investment Bank and on target prices from investment banks, according to The Star. Standard Investment Bank put the gap to its own fair value at KES 37.1 billion, Business Daily reports.

(Analysis) Against the market, KES 34 was a premium. But the buyer was not buying a few shares. It was buying control of the company, and the minority shareholders on the NSE got no offer at all. A premium of a fifth over a December close is the least the state could have asked for that.

## What the state gives up

(Facts) Safaricom paid KES 1.20 a share for the year to March 2025 and KES 2.00 for the year to March 2026, a record total of KES 80.13 billion. The register for the interim KES 0.85 closed on 25 February 2026, before the sale. The register for the final KES 1.15 closed on 4 August 2026, after it.

| On the 15% sold (6.01 billion shares) | KES |
| --- | --- |
| A year's dividend at KES 1.20 (year to March 2025) | 7.2bn |
| A year's dividend at KES 2.00 (year to March 2026) | 12.0bn |
| The interim the state kept (KES 0.85) | 5.1bn |
| The final that went to the buyer (KES 1.15) | 6.9bn |

(Facts) Business Daily puts the final dividend the state missed on all its 35% at about KES 16.1 billion. The dividend rights on the 20% it kept were worth KES 55.7 billion as paid, and were sold for KES 40.2 billion, a discount of 27.8%. Mr Mbadi said those future dividends were worth only KES 29.3 billion today at market rates, and that cash now beats an uncertain dividend later, according to the Nation. Vodacom's financial controller said Vodacom priced the advance at a return of 16.5% a year.

(Analysis) At last year's dividend of KES 2.00, the 15% yields about 5.9% a year on KES 34. The one-year Treasury bill sold on 12 October 2026 at 9.04%. Giving up a 5.9% yield to raise money the state would otherwise borrow at about 9% is reasonable arithmetic, if the dividend does not keep growing. It grew two-thirds last year.

(Analysis) The advance is the weaker part. If Vodacom earns 16.5% on it, the state borrowed KES 40.2 billion at a rate well above its own Treasury bill. Until Vodacom has recovered it, which Business Daily reports should take about three years, the state collects no dividend on its 20%.

## Who gains

(Analysis) Vodacom gets control of Safaricom. Within five weeks of the trade, the shares it bought qualified for the KES 1.15 final dividend, about KES 6.9 billion. The Treasury gets KES 244.5 billion in cash for its infrastructure fund. The NSE's minority shareholders keep their shares but lose the takeover offer the rules would normally have given them.

## Our verdict

(Opinion) **KES 34 beat the market on the day, but it was a soft price for control, and the dividend advance was dear money.**

The sale price was not a giveaway. It was well above where Safaricom traded when it was agreed, and the market was still below it in late June. The state had a fair case for cash now over dividends later.

But control is worth more than a block of shares, and here the buyer also skipped the offer to everyone else. The dividend advance asked the state to borrow at Vodacom's rate, not its own. And the sale was done in a way the High Court has now found broke the constitution. If the appeal fails, someone has to work out how KES 244.5 billion that has already gone into the infrastructure fund comes back.

### What would change our mind

The Treasury publishing the KCB Investment Bank valuation in full. A Safaricom dividend that falls rather than rises over the next two years. Or a Court of Appeal judgment that the public consultation was enough.
