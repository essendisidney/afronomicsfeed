## Post 1: savers vs borrowers

Two years ago, KES 100,000 in Kenya's one-year Treasury bill earned KES 14,220 a year after tax.

Today it earns KES 7,684.

The one-year bill fell from 16.73% (7 Oct 2024) to 9.04% (5 Oct 2026). Banks passed that on, but not evenly:

• Deposit rates: 11.14% → 6.91% (−4.23 points)
• Lending rates: 16.84% → 14.34% (−2.50 points)
• The gap banks keep: 5.70 → 7.43 points

Savers took the cut almost twice as hard as borrowers got the relief.

Two things you can do:
→ Saving? Judge every offer against the bill. A savings account pays 3.54% on average.
→ Borrowing? The bill and the industry average are on your side when you negotiate.

## Post 2: short bills

Kenyan investors are voting with their money: they want the 91-day bill, not the one-year.

Last five CBK auctions, bids as a share of the amount offered:

91-day: 428% · 370% · 291% · 222% · 225%
364-day: 22% · 130% · 94% · 112% · 106%

The one-year bill fell short of its offer twice in five weeks. On 7 September it drew KES 2.2bn of bids for KES 10bn.

Why? The extra time barely pays. The 91-day cleared at 8.77% on 5 October and the 364-day at 9.04%: 0.27 points more for locking money up four times as long.

What to watch: does the Treasury raise the one-year rate to fill its offer?
